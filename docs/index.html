Why Nakagawa

The goal: a real PC version of games you own.

It’s the same idea behind Zelda 64: Recompiled and Unleashed Recompiled, brought to the PlayStation Portable.

Runs natively

An emulator pretends to be a PSP while you play. Nakagawa turns the game into a program your PC runs directly.

Honest by design

When a game needs something Nakagawa can’t do yet, it stops and says what’s missing. It never fakes it.

Private and offline

No games or firmware are included or downloaded. Nothing made from your game leaves your PC, and there’s no tracking.

Open source

All the code is public under the GPL, and every change has to pass a large set of automatic checks before it’s accepted.

Works today

  • Drag in a disc image and the player recognises the game.
  • Encrypted discs are unlocked on your PC, the way PPSSPP does it.
  • One button turns the game into a PC program, with a progress bar and a cancel button.
  • Missing tools? The player can fetch them for you, after showing exactly what it will download.
  • Gamepads and keyboards, with button remapping and calibration, even per game.
  • Sound, video cutscenes and save files.

Not yet

  • A download. For now you build Nakagawa yourself (#278).
  • Anything but Windows 11. Linux, macOS and Android come later.
  • Playing straight from a disc image with no setup (#308).
  • Online or local wireless multiplayer (#1139).
  • Smooth speed and complete sound in Hot Shots Tennis (#1296).

Under the hood

Translated once. Played natively.

Nakagawa is a static recompiler: instead of imitating a PSP while you play, it rewrites the game for your PC before you start. Four steps, scored like a tennis game.

  1. 15

    Add your game

    Drop in the disc image of a game you own. Nakagawa reads the game’s name from the disc and checks that it can read the game’s code.

  2. 30

    Translate it

    The game was written for the PSP’s own processor. Nakagawa translates every part of it into C, a common programming language, just once.

  3. 40

    Build it

    A compiler turns that C into a Windows program, together with Nakagawa’s stand-in for the PSP’s system, graphics, sound and controls.

  4. Game

    Play

    Press Play. If the game reaches something Nakagawa can’t do yet, it stops and tells you what’s missing instead of guessing.

The matching decomp behind it

Hot Shots Tennis also has a project of its own: hst-decomp, a matching decompilation that rebuilds the game’s code as C and C++ that compiles back to the exact original bytes. On 27 October 2026 it had 35.9% of the game’s code matched (12,199 of 19,634 functions).

The two projects share findings, not code. The decomp’s function names can label the C that Nakagawa generates, and what it shows about the game helps replace Nakagawa’s Hot Shots Tennis workarounds with general fixes. No decomp source is copied into Nakagawa, which is meant for any PSP game, not just this one.

Before you play

Bring your own game.

Nakagawa is the software, not the game. Here’s what you’ll need once you can play.

  • A Windows 11 PC64-bit, with a graphics card that supports Vulkan, 8 GB of memory (16 GB is better) and 5 GB of free space.
  • Your own copy of the gameAs a disc image (.iso). Whether you may make one depends on where you live and the terms you agreed to, so check both.
  • Nothing else, for most gamesMost store-bought games keep their code locked. Nakagawa unlocks your own copy on your PC when you add it. See the note about unlocking.
  • Your PSP’s system fonts (optional)For the original lettering. Without them, Nakagawa uses its own open fonts.

We bring the court. You bring the game.

Unlocking happens on your PC

Most store-bought PSP games keep their code encrypted, which means locked. When you add your disc image, Nakagawa unlocks the game’s code on your PC with the same public game key tables the PPSSPP emulator ships. It never downloads games, never sends anything off your PC, and never touches PSP firmware.

Disc images work today; games bought as PlayStation Store downloads don’t yet. If a file can’t be unlocked, the player tells you which one, and you can add an unlocked copy made from your own game instead.

Rules about unlocking and copying games differ from country to country, so check yours. This isn’t legal advice. The guide to playing your own games has the details.

Questions

Good questions.

Can’t find yours? Ask in Discussions.

Is this an emulator?

No. An emulator imitates PSP hardware while the game runs. Nakagawa rewrites the game as a Windows program before you play, much like a PC port.

When can I download it?

Not yet, and there’s no date. The first release, v0.0.1, is being prepared, and you can follow it in issue #278. If you’re comfortable with developer tools, the README explains how to build it yourself.

Will my favourite game work?

Probably not yet. One game is far along, one plays a stage, and the rest haven’t been tried. When you add a game, the player tells you what it still needs.

Do you provide games or firmware?

No. Nothing here includes or downloads games, firmware or system fonts. You play your own copy of a game you own. To read an encrypted disc, Nakagawa uses the public game key tables the PPSSPP emulator also ships, on your PC only.

Does it go online?

Only if you say yes. The player works offline. The one exception today is an optional download of build tools, which it asks about first. Nothing made from your game is uploaded or shared, and there’s no tracking.

Who makes Nakagawa?

One maintainer, working with AI coding assistants. Every change is checked by people and by automatic tests before it’s accepted, and changes made with AI help say so. The AI usage policy has the rules.
